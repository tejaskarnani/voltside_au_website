Section 01

An independent rebate assessment business for Victorian households and small business

Voltside advertises to Victorians who are already looking to cut their energy bills, works out which of four overlapping government programs they qualify for, models what an upgrade would actually cost them after every rebate, and — with their explicit consent — introduces one accredited installer. We do not install, and we do not sell leads to a panel.

The thesis in three sentences

Subsidy design is doing the marketing. Federal solar certificates shrink every January until they vanish in 2030. The battery discount now steps down twice a year. The Solar Homes income threshold already fell from $210,000 to $150,000 on 1 July 2026. And from 1 January 2027 new Victorian homes cannot connect to gas at all. Every one of those is a real, dated, externally-imposed reason to act — which means this business never has to manufacture urgency.

Regulation has already killed the incumbent playbook. Cold-call telemarketing for Victorian Energy Upgrades products was banned in May 2024, door-knocking in August 2024, and buying someone's contact details from a third party is explicitly prohibited. The ACCC opened an in-depth review of lead generation itself in June 2025. The call-centre-and-a-list model that built the incumbent solar sales industry is no longer available.

That leaves consent-based digital inbound as the only durable route — which is a moat, not a limitation. A competitor cannot undercut you by doing something you've chosen not to do, because the thing they'd do is prohibited. The winning position is the one this industry has never credibly occupied: transparent numbers, exclusive leads, and a customer who was never contacted without asking.

What we're deliberately not doing

Not this

Not installing

Installation means electrical licensing, staff, vehicles, warranty exposure and working capital. The referral model stays asset-light and lets us stay genuinely product-neutral.

Not this

Not a comparison marketplace

Sending one enquiry to five installers is the practice that gave this industry its reputation. It also removes any reason for an installer to value your lead. One lead, one installer.

Not this

Not an authorised retailer — yet

Becoming a Solar Victoria authorised retailer requires NETCC signatory status and is slow and costly. Partner installers already hold it and lodge the rebates. Revisit once volume justifies it.

Not this

Not a finance broker

We show people what the interest-free Solar Homes loan and the federal green loan products offer, and stop there. Credit advice requires licensing we won't hold and incentives we don't want.

The one-line version. Buy high-intent search traffic, let people qualify themselves against real eligibility rules, hand one installer a customer who already understands their own numbers, and get paid twice — a little for the lead, properly for the install.

Section 03

Market and competition

Victoria is a large, subsidised, under-penetrated market experiencing a battery boom — and it is served by an industry with a genuinely poor reputation. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Where the demand sits

Roughly two-thirds of Victorian dwellings still have no rooftop solar, and regional Victoria runs ahead of Greater Melbourne on penetration — which tells you the constraint is housing stock and ownership, not appetite. Battery uptake has been transformed: national installations under the federal scheme ran more than twelve times the previous year's rate in the program's first eleven months, and Victoria contributed roughly 22,000 of them in under six months.

The more interesting market, though, is the one nobody is advertising into. Over two million Victorians use mains gas. Every one of those households will face a decision when their ducted heater or hot water unit fails, and from March 2027 rental properties will not be permitted to replace gas with gas at end of life. Replacing gas ducted heating is currently the single largest Victorian Energy Upgrades discount available to a household, and it carries no income test. That combination — large pool, big incentive, no eligibility screening, forced replacement moment — is structurally more attractive than the solar market everyone is fighting over.

The gap, stated plainly. Nobody in this market currently combines three things: genuine product neutrality (because we don't install), exclusive leads (because we refuse to share), and transparent modelling (because we publish our assumptions). Each is individually unremarkable. Together they answer the exact objection a Victorian homeowner brings to the first conversation, which is "how do I know you're not going to rip me off".

Why this industry has the reputation it does

These are the failure modes to design against, not just avoid:

  • The five phone calls. One web form, five installers, five sales calls in an afternoon. The customer stops answering unknown numbers for a week.
  • Headline rebate figures that don't apply. A big number in the ad, eligibility conditions in eight-point grey at the bottom. The ACCC has specifically flagged this pattern in lead generation.
  • Systems sized to the roof, not the bill. Bigger system, bigger commission — and with feed-in tariffs now unregulated and low, the extra export earns the customer almost nothing.
  • Savings claims nobody can substantiate. Confident annual figures with no stated assumptions, which then don't materialise.
  • Pressure selling to people who shouldn't buy. Renters, heavily shaded roofs, households about to move.
  • Finance bundled in opaquely. A monthly payment quoted instead of a price, with the credit terms discovered later.

Every one of these is a design decision we can invert cheaply — and each inversion is a marketing asset. "One installer, not five" is a compliance-safe, genuinely differentiated ad headline precisely because our competitors cannot honestly run it.

Section 04

Compliance and licensing

This section determines the shape of the business more than any other. Read it before the marketing plan, not after. Nothing here is legal advice — it is a map of what to take to an Australian lawyer before launch.

The four hard prohibitions in Victoria.
  • Cold-call telemarketing for Victorian Energy Upgrades products — banned since 1 May 2024. Express prior consent required before any marketing call.
  • Door-knocking — banned for VEU since 1 August 2024, and for Solar Homes since 2021.
  • Buying contact details from a third party and marketing to them — explicitly prohibited under the VEU rules.
  • Misleading rebate and savings claims — Australian Consumer Law, with the ACCC actively enforcing in this sector and Consumer Affairs Victoria having previously succeeded against a solar retailer over conduct by its third-party lead generators.

Do we need to be an authorised retailer?

To submit Solar Homes rebate applications on a customer's behalf and receive rebate payments through the retailer portal, a business must be an authorised Solar Victoria retailer, which requires being a signatory to the New Energy Tech Consumer Code and maintaining approved-seller status. Authorised retailers must also use accredited installers, hold appropriate electrical licensing for the work, and provide a whole-of-system warranty.

A pure referral business that does not install and does not lodge rebate applications does not itself need that authorisation — the partner installer, who is already an authorised retailer, lodges the rebate. That is the recommended launch structure. It is faster, cheaper, and keeps warranty and installation liability where the expertise sits. Revisit the question only if volume later justifies controlling the rebate lodgement process directly.

How consent is actually captured

Rule 1

Two boxes, separately

One consent to be contacted. A second, distinct consent to have details passed to one named installer. Bundling them into a single tick is the most common failure and the easiest to spot.

Rule 2

Never pre-ticked

Both start unticked. The person performs a positive act. Pre-ticked boxes are not consent.

Rule 3

Name the recipient

APP 6 disclosure works best when the customer knows who is receiving their information. We name the installer before we send anything.

Rule 4

Timestamp everything

An immutable consent ledger: what was ticked, when, from where, and the exact wording displayed at the time. This is the artefact that answers a regulator's question.

Rule 5

Withdrawal is easy and honoured

One-click unsubscribe on every message, a stated process for withdrawing contact consent, and a suppression list that is actually checked.

Rule 6

One installer, contractually

The exclusivity promise is written into the installer agreement, along with a prohibition on them on-selling the referral.

Before launch, take to a lawyer: the consent wording and privacy collection notice; the installer referral agreement including data-handling and exclusivity terms; the disclaimer set on the calculator; the substantiation file for every advertised claim; and a written assessment of whether the inbound model sits outside the ACL's unsolicited consumer agreement provisions.

The risk that actually kills this business. Section 08

Roadmap and expansion

Three stages in Victoria, then one state at a time — with a clear reason for the order.

Stage 1 · Months 0–3

Build the compliant foundation

Incorporate and insure. Sign two to four NETCC-authorised installers with written agreements covering exclusivity, conduct and data handling. Build the consent architecture and ledger. Legal review of consent wording, disclaimers and the referral agreement. Ship the calculator and four cornerstone rebate pages. Launch paid at $8,000/month against the Phase 1 gate.

Stage 2 · Months 3–6

Prove the unit economics

Scale to $18,000/month. Get offline conversion tracking live so both platforms optimise to installed jobs rather than form fills. Add a third installer. The gate to continue: CPL under $90, lead-to-appointment above 30%, appointment-to-sale above 20%. If those don't hold, fix conversion before adding spend.

Stage 3 · Months 6–12

Widen the front

$32,000/month. Open the commercial funnel — the SRES eligibility expansion to 1 MW makes this materially more attractive. Launch Performance Max now that clean conversion data exists. Build the builder and developer channel ahead of the January 2027 all-electric requirement, and the landlord channel ahead of the March 2027 rental standards.

Stage 4 · Months 12+

New South Wales

The strongest second market by some distance. Largest population, and the Home Energy Saver program opened in June 2026 with zero-interest loans up to $15,000 over ten years plus targeted discounts — a richer finance stack than Victoria currently offers. The Peak Demand Reduction Scheme and Energy Savings Scheme provide the VEU-equivalent certificate layer.
