The solar market, and where a lead-gen business actually fits in it.

Solar-only installs are shrinking. Battery installs tripled in a year. Roughly 270 solar companies have gone under. And the installer's margin on a typical job is thinner than most people assume. This is what that means for what we build, who we partner with, and what we can charge.

01

A market that's contracting and booming at the same time

Two things are true simultaneously: rooftop solar has never been bigger, and 2025 was still a down year for new installs. The reason is the same in both cases — the easy, high-payback rooftops are mostly already covered, and the growth has moved to the next product along: batteries.

National scale

By the end of 2025, Australia had 28.3 GW of rooftop solar across roughly 4.3 million systems (Clean Energy Council, H2 2025 report, published 4 Feb 2026), supplying 14.2% of national electricity in the second half of the year alone. 254,664 systems were installed across 2025 — down 15.2% on 2024's 300,375, and the first sub-300,000 year since 2020. IBISWorld puts the solar-panel-installation industry at roughly $2.3bn in 2026 revenue, down 14.7% year on year. SunWiz expects a rebound to around 350,000 installs across 2026, driven almost entirely by battery attach.

Why batteries, why now. The federal Cheaper Home Batteries Program launched 1 July 2025 — roughly a 30% upfront discount, delivered the same way as solar STCs. The program's cost blew out from an original estimate of ~$2.3bn to roughly $7.2bn. About half of 2025's battery installs paired with new or upgraded solar; about half were retrofits onto existing systems (CER, Q3 2025) — meaning the retrofit market is now roughly as large as the new-build attach market, and worth a dedicated funnel of its own.

Victoria specifically

Victoria has ~5.4 GW installed (third nationally behind NSW and Queensland), adding roughly 230 MW in H1 2025 alone. Roy Morgan put Victorian solar households at ~728,000 back in 2022. Victoria has the lowest average household electricity consumption of any state at ~4,615 kWh/year (AER) — a real ceiling on system size and savings relative to NSW or Queensland, and a reason Victorian jobs tend to sit at the smaller end of the national range.

Structure

Highly fragmented

No installer holds meaningful national share — even the most aggressive consolidator (1KOMMA5°) is small in absolute revenue terms. Favourable for an intermediary: no single gatekeeper to negotiate around.

Accreditation

2,000+ NETCC sellers

The New Energy Tech Consumer Code passed 2,000 approved sellers nationally in October 2025; 1,000+ are Victorian. Solar Accreditation Australia took over individual installer accreditation from the CEC on 29 May 2024.

The calendar

Rebates set the rhythm

The STC deeming period drops one year every 1 January (6→5 years on 1 Jan 2026), cutting roughly $300–$600 off a 6.6 kW system's discount each year until the scheme closes in 2030. The battery rebate begins tapering on 6-month intervals from 1 January 2027. Both reliably pull demand forward into December, then produce a lull.

The Origin/AGL split is the most useful data point in this section. Australia's two largest energy retailers looked at the same market and made opposite calls — one exited installing for referral, the other doubled down on bundling. Both can be right: Origin's 4.7 million-customer base makes referral volume trivial to generate; AGL's VPP strategy needs installed hardware it controls. A standalone lead-gen business is structurally closer to Origin's bet than AGL's.

(c) Local Victorian installers — the intended partners, not competitors

Typical profile: a small owner-operator or SME electrical/solar business, a handful of crews, a few hundred installs a year, revenue in the low single-digit millions. Many operate as, or under, a NETCC-approved retailer. Customer acquisition today is mostly word-of-mouth, local Google Business Profile/SEO, some purchased comparison-site leads, and a growing amount of paid social. Appetite for paid leads is real but quality-sensitive — they'll pay a real premium for exclusive, pre-qualified, rebate-verified, locally-relevant leads over shared marketplace leads, precisely because the shared model burns their sales time on enquiries they have a one-in-three chance of winning.

03

Profitability is thin, and the industry has a real failure problem

This is the section that should most shape who we partner with. Margins are real but not large, and the base rate of installer insolvency in Australia is high enough that partner selection is closer to a risk-management function than a sales one.

The insolvency problem

Scale

270+ collapses

Industry trackers document 270+ collapsed Australian solar companies (one aggregates 800+ ASIC actions since 2011). The Clean Energy Regulator estimates 600,000–700,000 "orphaned" systems whose workmanship warranties are effectively void.

Consumer impact

1 in 10 support requests

CHOICE: at least 10% of solar-owner help requests involve a liquidated company. In a survey of 1,000+ owners, 30% reported a problem with their solar company, and 4% couldn't get a warranty honoured at all.

Cause

Boom-bust margins

Thin margins, rebate-driven demand cycles, aggressive discounting ("$3,999 fully installed") and cashflow stress. Product warranties (panels, inverters) survive via the manufacturer; workmanship warranties die with the installer.

Implication for a referral model.
Referring a customer to a partner who later collapses doesn't just cost that customer a warranty — it costs the referring business its reputation, since the customer's memory of "who sent me there" outlasts their memory of "who I actually contracted with." Partner financial due diligence (ASIC checks, trading history, review trend, NETCC standing) is not optional overhead here; it is close to the core product.

What installers currently pay to acquire a customer, and what that implies for us

Shared comparison leads run $20–$50; exclusive $35–$120; commercial $150–$250. Overhead typically eats 15–25% of revenue. Given a $1,100–$1,400 gross margin on a standard solar-only job — and materially more on a battery job — a defensible referral fee sits at:

A note on how the fee is actually paid

Every figure above assumes the standard referral structure: the installer bills and collects from the customer, and pays us a fee per qualified lead or closed job. That structure requires nothing more than an ordinary commercial referral agreement.

If instead we collect payment from the customer directly and then pay the installer, we are legally operating as the solar retailer, not a referrer — energy.gov.au draws exactly this distinction. That changes the compliance floor materially: Solar Victoria requires authorised retailer status to submit Solar Homes rebate applications and receive rebate payments, and authorisation requires NETCC (New Energy Tech Consumer Code) signatory status — a process that is slow and shouldn't be assumed as a starting position. It also shifts the 5-year whole-of-system warranty and Australian Consumer Law liability onto us rather than the installer, and requires working capital to cover hardware and installer costs ahead of customer payment. The margin economics under that structure are different again — and generally better per job, but with substantially more risk — and sit outside the scope of this report.

What actually drives the decision, across every segment. Price and payback period dominate; trust and installer reputation are decisive at the point of choice, precisely because of the insolvency backdrop above; environmental motivation reinforces a decision already being made on financial grounds rather than driving it. The most common abandonment reason isn't price — it's quote fatigue from being contacted by three or four installers off one shared-lead enquiry, which is the exact failure mode an exclusive-lead model is built to remove.

06

Where the defensible position actually is

Given a fragmented installer base, a confusing four-program rebate stack, and an industry with a genuine trust problem, the opening isn't in out-bidding SolarQuotes for search traffic — it's in being the party that makes the rebate stack legible and stands behind who it refers.

Wedge

Rebate-qualification depth

The stacked rebate landscape confuses consumers and is largely unaddressed by generic "3 free quotes" comparison sites. Pre-qualifying against the exact stack — income cap, property value, existing solar, battery suitability — is the differentiator.

Wedge

Exclusive over shared

Incumbents are structurally locked into the shared model because it's how they scale cheaply. An exclusive or two-installer-max lead removes the quote-fatigue problem that is this industry's single most common objection.

Wedge

Battery-first positioning

Solar-only volume is shrinking; battery volume tripled. A funnel built around battery-attach and battery-retrofit leads targets the highest-value jobs and the most motivated, least price-sensitive buyers.

Wedge

Partner curation as brand

Given 270+ collapses, actively vetting for financial health and NETCC status — and dropping partners who slip — is itself a trust-based moat competitors relying on volume can't easily copy.

Realistic addressable market

Victoria's ~18% share of the national ~221,000 battery installs in 2025 implies roughly 40,000 Victorian battery installs, plus a further tranche of solar-only systems. Capturing even 1–3% of Victorian purchase intent implies a few hundred to low-thousands of qualified leads per year. At $60–$150 per qualified lead plus success fees, that's plausibly a seven-figure revenue line within a few years — an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a bottom-up forecast, and highly sensitive to actual conversion and fee realisation.

Recommendations

1
Launch battery-and-rebate-led, exclusive-lead funnels first

Prioritise solar+battery and battery-retrofit leads. Sell exclusive or two-installer-max, rebate-verified, appointment-booked leads at $60–$150, with a success fee on close. If partner close rates stay above ~25–30%, hold premium pricing; below ~15%, tighten qualification or cut price.

2
Curate a small panel of financially sound, NETCC-approved installers

Active due diligence — ASIC checks, trading history, review trend. Cap leads per installer to protect close rates and diversify collapse risk. Drop any partner with rising complaint volume.

3
Build the rebate-eligibility engine as core IP

Screen postcode/STC zone, ownership, the $150,000 income cap (from 1 July 2026), property value, existing solar and battery suitability, then book a consultation. This is the differentiator versus a generic comparison form.

4
Exploit the rebate calendar deliberately

Run demand-capture campaigns ahead of each 1 January STC step-down and each battery-rebate taper from 2027. Manage installer capacity ahead of time to avoid the post-deadline lull damaging close rates.

5
Diversify ahead of the 2030 STC wind-down

Batteries/VPP, heat-pump hot water, EV chargers, and energy-plan or finance referral (SolarQuotes-style ancillary fees of $16–$50) all extend the model past the point solar rebates stop being a hook.

6
Track leading indicators monthly

Victorian install and battery volumes (CEC/SunWiz), step-down dates, partner close and complaint rates, and CPL/CPA trend. Shift budget away from any channel whose CPA exceeds roughly a third of the fee it earns.

Threats

Threat

Installer disintermediation

As installers build owned/organic channels targeting $0–$50 leads, they buy fewer paid ones. Lead quality has to stay high enough to remain worth paying for.

Threat

Platform competition

SolarQuotes (Origin-backed), Solar Choice and a wave of exclusive-lead brokers compete for the same installers and the same search/social traffic. SEO and paid-search costs are rising.

Threat

Regulatory wind-down

STC scheme ends 2030; battery rebate tapers from 2027; Solar Victoria's income cap already tightened once. Each step-down is a spike then a trough, and the long-run trend erodes the rebate-qualification hook.

Threat

Rising compliance scrutiny

The Clean Energy Regulator inspected 1,278 Cheaper Home Battery installs between July 2025 and April 2026 and found 60.8% "substandard" and 1.2% "unsafe." Referring to a poor installer now carries real reputational and potential legal exposure.

07

Methodology, sourcing and caveats

Read this before quoting a figure from this report externally.

Sources prioritised
Clean Energy Council, Clean Energy Regulator, APVI, SunWiz, ABS, AER, IBISWorld, ACCC, Solar Victoria, the Essential Services Commission, plus reputable industry sources (SolarQuotes, PV Magazine Australia, RenewEconomy, One Step Off The Grid, Solar Insiders) for pricing and market data where official figures don't exist.
Figures that need verification before external use
  • IBISWorld's business-count figures (5,046 in 2026 vs 8,505 in 2025) come from search-snippet summaries; the scale of that one-year drop should be checked against the full report.
  • Solargain and Smart Energy Group financials are third-party estimates or dated (2017–19), not audited.
Where numbers are indicative rather than exact
  • Cost-stack component prices are indicative trade/market ranges; actual costs vary by volume, brand and site. The 6.6 kW gross-margin figure is a synthesised estimate, not a single published number.
  • The battery-job cost composition in the calculator above does not reconcile to one figure in the underlying research — treat the gap between cost and retail as directional, not exact.
  • Battery attach-rate and average-size figures rely on SunWiz reporting, which uses several different definitions (as-proposed vs as-sold) across sources.
  • Cumulative Cheaper Home Batteries Program install counts vary by reporting date across sources (100,000 by Oct 2025; 250,000+ / 6.3 GWh later; 284,000 by May 2026; 500,000+ in a later announcement) — always cite with the date attached.
  • The total-addressable-market estimate in Section 06 is order-of-magnitude, not a bottom-up market study.
Currency
Figures are current to approximately August 2026. The rebate landscape changes frequently — STC step-downs each January, the battery-rebate taper from 2027, and the Solar Victoria income cap change of 1 July 2026 are all scheduled or recent changes. Re-verify before acting on any specific number.
